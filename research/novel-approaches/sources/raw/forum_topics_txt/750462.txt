# Early Qwen layers know the author better than late ones: 0.39 vs 0.31
url: https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/name-that-llm/discussion/750462
author: Aleksey Osipenko date: 2026-10-18 votes: {'totalVotes': 9, 'canUpvote': True, 'totalUpvotes': 10}
Most embedding approaches use the last hidden layer. As it turned out, for this task the last layer was the least effective one.

Setup: a frozen Qwen3-4B model (36 layers), mean pooling, and logistic regression on each layer; cross-validation split by `prompt_id`. In all my submissions this CV has been consistently about 0.015 above the public leaderboard, so I trust it for comparisons.

Balanced accuracy by layer:
- Layer 3 (8% depth): 0.391
- Layer 6: 0.379
- Layer 9: 0.370
- Layer 18: 0.327
- Layer 27: 0.341
- Layer 36 (last layer): 0.311
- All 6 layers concatenated: 0.414

In my view, deeper layers encode what the text is about, while earlier layers capture how it is written – word choice, punctuation and rhythm. Authorship lives in the style, and the topic acts as a trap, especially since the test prompts are completely new.

Combined with a TF-IDF + stylometry model (0.369 on its own), the blend reached 0.439 CV and 0.420 on the public leaderboard. With only 4 layers (9, 18, 27 and 36), the same blend gave 0.429 CV and 0.414 on the leaderboard. This result has helped shape my plan for further work.

The task is still challenging: it is hard to tell apart related models from the same lab, for example `claude-fable-5` (recall 0.11), `gpt-5.6-sol` (0.21) and the GLM models (~0.21). I need to give more thought to the right approach here.
